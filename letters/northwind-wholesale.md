## Letter

Dear Andrew Weston,

The sentence about solutions that must still work when someone comes back from a skiing holiday is the best line I have read in a job posting this year. It says you already know where AI projects die: not in the pilot, but on the morning nobody is there to restart something. That kind of operation is what I have spent the last years on. As lead AI architect at The Northport Courier I run a production pipeline on Linux and systemd timers, built to run unattended rather than to look good in a demo.

I build myself, every day: editorial AI agents with specialised roles, automatic article generation with built-in source grounding, internal RAG for semantic search, and a monitoring system where every claim can be traced back to its source. Without traceability such a system is not a basis for decisions but noise with confidence, and in a wholesale business, where the text about a product, a price or a delivery time is a claim the company is liable for, that is not technical decoration but the precondition.

Distribution logic is not new to me. I have built RAG-based text generation into a large leasing company's existing fleet management system. The pattern is the same whatever the industry: structured product data that exists, plus tacit knowledge with the experienced staff that does not. The good use cases sit in the gap between the two, and they are found by talking to the people who do the work, not by reading a system diagram.

Most of a well-functioning AI system is not AI. It is rules, routing, validation and control, and I write that layer so that it is readable by both the business and your IT colleagues instead of hiding inside a model. That makes the architecture, security and compliance conversation with IT concrete rather than principled, and I prefer to build on what already runs rather than tear it down.

I would like to hear what the first use cases at Northwind look like, and I can make both interview dates in August.

## Closing

Kind regards,

**Robin Vale**
